For the default chip the model's best setting is 1.75 mL/min (clean window about 1.60–1.85). There red and white cells finish their second Dean cycle and are back at the outer wall as they reach the outlets, while tumour cells stay focused at the inner wall. A narrower window sits near 0.9 mL/min, after one cycle. Between and beyond these, the small cells arrive out of phase.

What is and isn't modeled
  • Design sliders extrapolate. The model was tuned on the 500 × 130 µm chip. Trends from changing the design are indicative, and least reliable as the channel approaches a square cross-section.
  • Geometry is to scale. Cells are drawn larger than life only when the camera is too far away to resolve them.
  • Dean flow is computed for the actual cross-section: the leading-order secondary flow at low Dean number, solved on a grid each time the design changes. It scales with ρU²/μR and includes the outward shift of the main flow's velocity peak. Above De ≈ 17 an estimated roll-off slows its growth toward the De1.63 trend seen in full simulations.
  • Lift migration velocity scales as fLρUmax²a³ / 3πμh² (Di Carlo), with fL = 0.035 at Re 50 falling as Re−0.5. That one number was chosen by scoring against Martel & Toner's 2012 spiral experiments. The shape of the lift field is constructed, not simulated: it has the equilibria known for straight rectangular channels (0.6 of the half-height from the centre plane, near the middle of each wall) and acts within half a channel height of a wall.
  • Cells are rigid spheres with ±10% size spread, no cell–cell interaction, plus a small random dispersion term. Red cells use their 5.6 µm volume-equivalent diameter.
  • Concentration is roughly 10⁴ times lower than whole blood, and tumour cells are far more common here than in a real sample.
  • Exosomes (about 100 nm, drawn enlarged) feel no meaningful lift and simply trace the Dean vortices.
